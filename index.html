01 — Overview

Donki is more than a Japanese store.

DON DON DONKI is the overseas format of Pan Pacific International Holdings' Don Quijote retail system. Its proposition combines Japanese products, discount pricing, convenience and an intentionally chaotic "treasure hunt" shopping experience.

The central question is therefore not simply whether Japanese culture is popular. The more useful question is whether Donki's complete business system fits the market: its products, prices, locations, promotions, operations and ability to generate repeat purchases.

Singapore and Malaysia provide a useful comparison because the same Japanese retail concept operates within very different consumer, income, competitive and cultural environments.

02 — Market Study

The market is not one customer.

Donki operates in FMCG, groceries, perishables, beauty and lifestyle retail. This means it competes simultaneously with premium supermarkets, discount retailers, specialist stores and increasingly convenient online platforms.

A useful market study therefore needs to examine the customer segments Donki can realistically serve rather than treating "Malaysian consumers" or "Singaporean consumers" as one group.

THE DIGITAL THREAT

Donki's biggest competitor may not be another supermarket.

Online grocery platforms reduce the value of physically visiting a store. Customers can search for exactly what they want, compare prices and receive products without travelling.

Donki therefore needs to maintain a reason to physically visit: discovery, exclusivity, freshness, food experience and products that are difficult to replicate through a search bar.

THE SENSODYNE QUESTION

If a product sells well, why not localise the supply chain?

If Donki can identify a high-volume product that customers repeatedly purchase, it could investigate local sourcing, regional production or larger supplier agreements. This can lower logistics costs and reduce exposure to exchange rates.

MALAYSIA CASE STUDY

Mid Valley: strong mall ≠ automatically optimal network

Mid Valley Megamall provides a strong example of the importance of location. High footfall can support discovery and tourism, but Kuala Lumpur also contains many competing malls and retail formats. The strategic question is therefore not simply "where is the busiest mall?" but "where is there enough profitable Donki demand that is not already being served?"

What would a better Malaysian network look like? JB? Penang? Other high-income urban catchments?
10 — Cultural Localisation

Localisation is not the removal of Japanese identity.

🕌

Halal

Malaysia's Muslim-majority market makes halal availability commercially important. The correct question is not whether Donki has "no halal products", but whether the halal range is sufficient to make the overall proposition accessible to the target market.

🍜

Food Culture

Japanese food can remain central while the assortment also reflects local preferences, dietary requirements and familiar flavours.

🏷️

Communication

Japanese visual identity should remain distinctive, but promotions, signage and product explanations can be adapted to local customers.

👥

Management

Local teams should have enough autonomy to respond quickly to customer behaviour, suppliers and local competition.

12 — Store Economics

Can a Donki store actually make money?

A country can have a large population, strong economic growth and high Japanese cultural interest and still be a bad market if individual stores cannot cover their fixed costs.

14 — International Expansion

Where should Donki go next?

ASEAN should not be treated as one market. The same Donki format could perform differently in Thailand, Vietnam, Indonesia, Malaysia and Singapore because consumer behaviour, purchasing power, logistics, religion and retail structures differ significantly.

🇸🇬

Singapore

MATURE / OPTIMISE

High purchasing power, strong Japanese exposure and dense urban catchments. Focus on productivity rather than simply increasing store count.

🇲🇾

Malaysia

SELECTIVE

Large market but stronger price sensitivity and a greater need for halal and local assortment. Focus on profitable catchments rather than network size.

🇹🇭

Thailand

TEST & SCALE

Existing Donki presence and strong Japanese cultural and tourism links provide a useful benchmark for localisation.

🇻🇳

Vietnam

PILOT

Investigate Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City first. Test modern retail demand, Japanese product interest, logistics and rent.

🇮🇩

Indonesia

HIGH POTENTIAL / HIGH LOCALISATION

Huge potential scale, but Muslim-majority consumers, geography, halal requirements and traditional retail require substantial localisation.

15 — Management Recommendations

What should Donki management do?

01

Build catchment-specific stores

Stop assuming one store format fits every location. Use local demographics, income, footfall and competition to determine assortment and format.

02

Separate discovery from routine

Keep Japanese exclusives as the reason to discover Donki, but increase everyday grocery products that generate repeat purchasing.

03

Localise key-value pricing

Benchmark high-frequency products against local competitors instead of relying entirely on imported Japanese pricing.

04

Strengthen local sourcing

Where demand is proven, negotiate larger supplier volumes or explore regional production to reduce logistics costs.

05

Measure repeat behaviour

Track repeat visits, basket size, SKU productivity and customer segments rather than relying on footfall alone.

06

Do not expand for the sake of scale

Every new location should pass a store-level economic test before the next phase of expansion.

FINAL JUDGEMENT

DONKI SHOULD NOT EXPORT ONE STORE MODEL.

It should export a business system that can be adapted to each market.
