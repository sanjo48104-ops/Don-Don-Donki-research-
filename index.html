01 — Introduction

The Question

DON DON DONKI is PPIH's Southeast Asian Japan-brand specialty-store format. PPIH describes it as a store offering products mostly made in Japan or designed for the Japanese market. The first DON DON DONKI in Singapore opened in 2017.

The current PPIH network is a useful starting point, but store count is not proof of success by itself. As of 19 July 2026, PPIH listed 15 stores in Singapore, 2 in Malaysia, 8 in Thailand, 10 in Hong Kong and 6 in Taiwan. The research therefore focuses on the conditions that could explain different market outcomes rather than assuming that one country simply "likes Japan" more.

Research Question

Why has DON DON DONKI developed a much larger store network in Singapore than Malaysia, and what does this suggest about product, price, place and promotion decisions when entering other markets?

02 — Product

Is the Product Actually Different?

Product is the first thing to test. PPIH positions DON DON DONKI as a Japan-brand specialty store, so its advantage should come from the assortment: products made in Japan, products designed for the Japanese market, and products that are difficult to find through ordinary supermarkets.

That does not mean every imported product is a competitive advantage. If the same SKU is widely available elsewhere, Japanese origin alone does not make Donki differentiated.

🎌

Japan Exposure: Test It

Singapore had 726,200 visitors to Japan in 2025. JNTO also describes Singapore as having many repeat visitors and strong familiarity with Japanese culture. This supports the hypothesis that Singapore consumers may be more familiar with Japanese products, but it does not prove that this caused Donki's performance.

Test: compare Japan travel exposure with DONKI sales or footfall across Singapore, Kuala Lumpur, Johor Bahru and Penang. If the relationship appears repeatedly, the hypothesis becomes stronger.

🛍️

Measure Product Exclusivity

Create a sample of 20–30 important SKUs. Put each into one of three groups: A only available through Donki, B available at specialist/import retailers, or C widely available at supermarkets and online.

Then compare sales, price and margin across the groups. This tells you whether Donki's differentiation is actually generating value.

🔁

Repeat Customers & CLV

Customer lifetime value should not be invented from a guess. Ideally collect customer frequency, average basket, gross margin and retention period.

If Donki data is unavailable, show a scenario model: CLV ≈ average basket × purchase frequency × gross-margin rate × expected retention period. Label every assumption clearly.

🏪

Like-for-Like Competition

Singapore comparisons should include FairPrice, FairPrice Finest, Sheng Siong and Cold Storage, plus online grocery. Malaysia comparisons should include AEON, Lotus's, Jaya Grocer and Village Grocer, plus online grocery.

Compare the same things: price, Japanese assortment, everyday groceries, convenience, delivery, store location and shopping experience.

🏷️

Build a Price Basket

Choose identical SKUs sold by Donki and competitors. Record prices on the same day, including promotions, pack size and unit price. Calculate Donki price ÷ competitor price for every item, then compare the median.

💰

Willingness to Pay

Do not decide that a price is affordable from personal opinion. Survey consumers or use existing research. Ask for the maximum price they would pay and report the median. Separate Japanese-exclusive products from ordinary products.

🚚

Supply Chain & Sourcing

PPIH's overseas model makes sourcing a key operational question. Investigate supplier → importer → distributor → store costs. If Donki can remove an intermediary or source locally, estimate the saving per unit instead of assuming the retail price should automatically fall.

📊

Margin vs Market Share

If sourcing lowers unit cost by S$1, management has choices: keep the price and gain S$1 gross profit, cut the price and potentially increase volume, or split the saving. Use contribution margin = selling price − variable cost and model different sales volumes.

04 — Place

Where Should Donki Be?

A good mall is not automatically a good Donki location. The question is whether the catchment contains enough target customers to generate the sales needed to cover rent, labour, logistics and other fixed costs.

📍

Catchment Analysis

For every candidate store, collect population within the catchment, household expenditure, mall footfall, transport access, tourism, competitor density and Japanese-product demand. Compare these variables against actual store sales where possible.

🇲🇾

KL Is Not Malaysia

Compare Kuala Lumpur, Johor Bahru and Penang instead of using one national average. DOSM reports 2024 mean monthly household consumption expenditure of RM8,178 in Kuala Lumpur, RM5,927 in Johor and RM5,850 in Penang, against RM5,566 nationally.

🕌

Halal Is a Real Market Constraint

JAKIM states that imported food and goods marketed as halal must comply with Malaysian requirements or be certified by a foreign halal certification body recognised by JAKIM. Imported meat and meat products have additional certification and inspection requirements.

Measure: what percentage of Donki's food assortment can realistically serve Muslim customers, and what sales could be lost or gained by changing that assortment?

🏢

Local Management

Do not assume regional headquarters and local stores make the same decisions. Investigate who controls assortment, pricing, promotions, suppliers and site selection. The useful question is whether local information actually changes the store model.

📉

Do Not Use Store Count as Proof

PPIH's current network lists 15 Singapore stores and 2 Malaysia stores, but store count alone cannot tell you revenue, profitability or customer loyalty. Track openings, closures, store age and store-level performance before drawing conclusions.

🧮

Calculate Break-Even

For a proposed store: required revenue = fixed costs ÷ contribution margin ratio. Another useful check is revenue = customers per day × average basket × operating days. Compare the required customer count with realistic footfall and conversion assumptions.

3

Management question: instead of asking "Was Mid Valley a bad location?", ask "Could this catchment generate enough contribution margin to cover the store's fixed costs, and is another Malaysian catchment better?"

05 — Promotion

What Is the Value Proposition?

Promotion should not just make Donki visible. It should tell the target customer why Donki is worth choosing over a supermarket, specialist retailer or delivery platform.

TEST THE PROPOSITION

Japan + Value + Discovery

🎌
Japanese products

PPIH itself positions DON DON DONKI around Japanese products. Promotion can therefore sell authenticity and access to products that customers may otherwise have to buy in Japan or through specialist importers.

💴
Value

Price needs to be demonstrated with the same-SKU price basket. If Donki is not cheaper, promotion should explain the extra value rather than simply using discount language.

🔎
Discovery

The store experience can create a reason to browse. The test is whether browsing produces larger baskets and repeat visits, not whether the store looks interesting.

TEST THE FIT

Local relevance + Value + Access

🛒
Everyday value

Compare the actual basket against Malaysian competitors before claiming Donki is too expensive or too cheap.

🕌
Broader food access

Halal availability can affect the addressable customer base. Promotion cannot solve an assortment problem on its own.

📱
Measure conversion

Compare campaign reach with store traffic, first-time visits, average basket and repeat visits. Social-media attention is not the same as customer retention.

📣

Customer Acquisition

Track campaign reach → store visits → first purchase. If these numbers are unavailable, state the limitation instead of assuming a campaign worked because it received views.

🔁

Customer Retention

Track repeat visits, purchase frequency, basket size and membership/app activity where possible. The goal is to convert Japanese curiosity into a shopping habit.

⚖️

Cost Minimisation vs Service

Donki has to decide what it is selling: the lowest possible price, a wide Japanese assortment, a discovery experience, or a combination. The target segment should determine the mix.

06 — Expansion

Where Should Donki Go Next?

Donki should not enter a country because the market is large or Japanese culture is popular. It should first test whether the four Ps can produce a viable store economics model.

Existing overseas markets are useful comparison cases: PPIH currently lists 8 stores in Thailand, 10 in Hong Kong and 6 in Taiwan. These markets can help separate factors that travel well from factors that need localisation.

🇹🇭

Thailand

Thailand is already an established Donki market. Study store-level sales, local sourcing, product mix and promotions across Bangkok and outside Bangkok. The key question is whether the same model works beyond the most internationally exposed areas.

🇭🇰

Hong Kong

Hong Kong is a useful comparison because it has a high level of Japanese retail exposure but a different cost and competitive environment. Compare Japanese-product demand, sourcing and store economics with Singapore.

🇹🇼

Taiwan

Taiwan can test the cultural-exposure hypothesis. If familiarity with Japan is important, compare customer demand and repeat purchasing with Singapore and Hong Kong rather than assuming cultural affinity guarantees success.

🇻🇳

Vietnam: Proposed Entry Test

Score each candidate city on Japanese-product demand, household spending, price sensitivity, competition, logistics, regulation, store rent, footfall and potential gross margin. Only recommend entry if the estimated revenue can support the required investment.

🌏

Other Regions

Use Taiwan, Hong Kong, Thailand and the US as comparison cases rather than calling any market a success or failure without store-level evidence. The useful question is: which parts of the model transfer, and which need localisation?

🧮

Expansion Gate

Before approval: 1) market fit, 2) product differentiation, 3) competitive price, 4) suitable site, 5) supply-chain feasibility, 6) break-even case. If one is weak, management should test it before committing capital.

07 — Management Recommendation

What Should Donki Management Do?

The main recommendation is to stop treating Singapore and Malaysia as a simple cultural comparison. Singapore's higher Japanese travel exposure is a plausible hypothesis, but it needs to be tested against actual Donki demand. Malaysia also contains different markets, so Kuala Lumpur should not automatically represent Johor Bahru or Penang.

For Malaysia, management should run a city-by-city catchment and store-economics comparison. The analysis should combine household spending, footfall, competitor density, Japanese-product demand, halal suitability, expected basket size and contribution margin. If another city produces a stronger break-even case, expansion should move there instead of simply adding another Kuala Lumpur location.

For product and price, management should build a common SKU basket across countries. This shows whether Donki is genuinely cheaper, whether its exclusive products justify a premium, and where supply-chain changes could lower costs. Savings should not automatically be passed entirely to customers: the decision should balance volume, market share and contribution margin.

For future markets such as Vietnam, Donki should use Thailand, Hong Kong, Taiwan and Singapore as comparison cases. The goal is not to copy one country's format. It is to identify which parts of the model consistently create sales and which need localisation.
